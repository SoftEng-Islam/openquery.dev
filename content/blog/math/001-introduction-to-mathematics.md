Ask a room full of people which school subject they dreaded most, and plenty of hands will shoot up for math. If yours is one of them, welcome! You're in very good company, and I'm glad you're here.

Here's a secret, though: you've already done math today. You figured out how long you had before leaving the house. You checked whether that discount was really a good deal. Maybe you split a bill with friends: 240 divided by 4 is 60 each. That's math, and you did it without a textbook, and probably without even thinking about it.

## Math You Already Use

Math sneaks into our everyday decisions all the time:

- **In the kitchen:** a recipe serves 4 and needs 2 cups of rice. Cooking for 6? That's 3 cups.
- **On the clock:** you leave at 8:15 and the trip takes 40 minutes, so you'll arrive at 8:55.
- **With money:** you have 500 and spend 130, which leaves you with 370.

Nothing fancy here, just addition, subtraction, and a little confidence. But math goes far beyond daily life. It's a language for describing patterns and relationships. Once we can describe something precisely, we can measure it, predict it, and build with it. That's why computers run on it, and why scientists use it to describe everything from the tiniest particles to the biggest galaxies.

So what exactly *is* this thing we call math, and where does it come from? Let's find out.

## Where Does Math Come From?

Objects in the night sky follow mathematical laws of motion whether or not we ever write those laws down. Because of this, many mathematicians believe that mathematical truths have a kind of existence of their own, independent of us and our symbols.

Take one plus one equals two. It doesn't depend on two people each dropping an apple into a basket and agreeing that they now have two apples. It's simply true. It was true before any human was born, and it will still be true long after we're gone.

So where does the math we meet in class come from? Did some caveman stumble across a calculus textbook buried under a rock? Was it handed down from the heavens? If math has ever felt like that to you, a mysterious thing that everyone else just *gets*, I promise you're not alone.

The real story is much more interesting. The truths are *discovered*, but the tools are *invented*. The notation, the methods, and the questions we choose to ask all come from people like us. And it isn't just ancient history: right now, thousands of mathematicians around the world are creating brand-new math.

## What Math Really Is

To understand how we create math, it helps to know what math is. Here's the short version: math is the study of questions that have definite answers.

That sounds almost too broad, and it is! To be a bit more precise, these are questions whose answers can be settled by pure logic, with no opinions or measurements needed. That's what makes math stand apart from other kinds of inquiry.

### Some questions don't have definite answers

- What is the best form of government?
- Who deserves the Oscar for Best Picture?
- Which is better, pizza or cheeseburgers?

(Fun to argue about, right?) The answers to these are subjective. They change from person to person, and nobody can give a definite answer, because there simply isn't one.

Even science works a little differently. If we repeat an experiment again and again, and our equations keep predicting the result correctly, we can feel very confident that we understand what's going on. But we can never be one hundred percent sure the next result won't surprise us. Science can be reliable far beyond reasonable doubt, yet it never claims complete certainty.

### Math is different

Try this: draw a circle and look for the angle between a radius and a tangent line. You'll find ninety degrees, or you'll have made a mistake. There's no in-between.

Every mathematical question like this has a completely clear-cut answer:

- **Are there infinitely many prime numbers?** Yes, definitely.
- **Is there a fraction that, multiplied by itself, gives exactly two?** No, definitely not. We'll see why together in this course.

A mathematician's job is to ask questions like these, then invent the tools needed to answer them. The answers themselves are fixed and out of our hands. They're woven into the fabric of the reality we live in, and they're often wonderfully surprising. Is there more than one kind of infinity? Yes! In fact, there are infinitely many different kinds of infinity.

Humans didn't decide that this was true. We just got curious enough to ask the question.
